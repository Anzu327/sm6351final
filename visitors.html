Reading the archive

Meteorites: historical NASA catalogue snapshot, 860–2013. Of 45,716 records, 32,186 have usable coordinates. Acquisition in 2026 does not mean the catalogue is complete through 2026. Zero-zero placeholders and invalid coordinates are not mapped. Mass is catalogue material mass, not entry mass. Found years describe discovery, not an observed atmospheric arrival.

Fireballs: NASA/JPL snapshot, 1988–2026; 1,074 records, 888 with coordinates. Positions are peak brightness locations; energy is kt TNT impact energy. Missing locations remain searchable. These layers are different kinds of evidence and are not a map of impact risk.

Animated arrivals

Orange short trails adapt the motion in Mappa's Animated Meteorite Landings to the globe. Only the 1,096 located records marked Fell are animated in the unfiltered meteorite view. Each trail ends at its catalogue coordinates; incoming paths and playback order are illustrative, not measured trajectories or a historical timeline. Head size, tail length, brightness, and landing flash scale with recovered catalogue mass on a fixed logarithmic scale. Larger records add a restrained expanding halo; records without a mass are neutral grey. The catalogue does not supply impact energy for these falls: landing glows are illustrative accents, not measured impact severity. A landing briefly flashes, then leaves a small mark. PAUSE freezes the motion. Open the records to inspect or filter the full catalogue. The fireball layer continues to show atmospheric observations rather than ground landings.

Models, art & motion

The Chelyabinsk specimen uses the project’s illustrated model. Scroll past the stone and through three rings to reach the solar system. The city uses the project's painted winter concept and an exaggerated, scroll-driven fireball; the extra building and branch meshes have been removed. It is an artistic reconstruction. The solar system compresses distances and enlarges bodies; particles and paths are schematic, not a reconstruction of the 2013 object's orbit.

On 15 February 2013, a fireball crossed the sky over the southern Urals. The meteorite’s main mass fell into Lake Chebarkul, west of Chelyabinsk.

Surface and interior

Many recovered stones have a fresh black or brown fusion crust. Inside the fragments, researchers found both light rock with visible chondrules and dark, fine-grained rock melted by an impact.
