    Reading the archive

    Meteorites: an October 2024 MetBull snapshot from the SweetEyesBirb public mirror. Of 83,625 source entries, 82,854 are retained (76,372 Official, 6,385 Provisional, 97 Relict*); 771 pseudo, doubtful, crater, discredited, undocumented, or artifact entries are excluded. Naming status stays visible on selected records. There are 50,951 usable locations and 31,903 records without usable coordinates. Missing and invalid coordinates remain unmapped. Some locations are approximate or shared collection sites. This is a historical catalogue snapshot, not the latest official export. Mass is recovered material mass, not entry mass or impact energy. Found years describe discovery, not observed arrival.

    Fireballs: NASA/JPL snapshot, 1988–2026; 1,074 records, 888 with coordinates. Positions are peak brightness locations; energy is kt TNT impact energy. Missing locations remain searchable. These layers are different kinds of evidence and are not a map of impact risk.

    Animated arrivals & regional counts

    Short blue and pink trails sample representative records from each batch; every mapped record contributes once to its regional count. The complete selection is revealed in about 30 seconds of playback, and replaying trails does not inflate totals. Flight paths, batch order, and landing glows are illustrative: Found records are not presented as witnessed events. Trail size and landing brightness vary with recovered mass. The resting points for unknown masses are grey.

    Records are grouped into H3 cells at resolution 4. Line widths use roughly 80% of each cell’s diameter. Small counts stay close to the surface and larger counts produce longer lines, with the tallest clusters compressed to fit the globe view. Colour runs from golden yellow through orange to red by log count. These are catalogue record counts, not independent impacts or global fall rates. SHOW ALL COUNTS skips the reveal and displays the entire mapped selection immediately. Open the records to search or filter; closing the archive reveals the current filtered selection.

    Meteorite density map

    After the Earth chapter, the globe turns and opens into an Equal Earth map. It uses a spherical Gaussian kernel at 0.9° bandwidth and a colour ramp from blue-violet through cyan and green to yellow and red. Each of the 50,951 located meteorite records contributes one unit. The map covers the full catalogue, including finds, and uses a logarithmic colour scale. It shows the geographic concentration of catalogue records.

    Models, art & motion

    The Chelyabinsk specimen uses the project’s illustrated model. In the integrated story, explore the specimen in the analysis diagram before the reconstruction. Scroll through three rings to reach the solar system. The city uses the project's painted winter concept and an exaggerated, scroll-driven fireball; the extra building and branch meshes have been removed. It is an artistic reconstruction. The solar system compresses distances and enlarges bodies; particles and paths are schematic, not a reconstruction of the 2013 object's orbit.

    On 15 February 2013, a fireball crossed the sky over the southern Urals. The meteorite’s main mass fell into Lake Chebarkul, west of Chelyabinsk.

    Surface and interior

    Many recovered stones have a fresh black or brown fusion crust. Inside the fragments, researchers found both light rock with visible chondrules and dark, fine-grained rock melted by an impact.
